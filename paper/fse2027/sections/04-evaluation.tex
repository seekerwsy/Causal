\section{Evaluation Design and Results}

The evaluation is organized around the title question. RQ1 identifies
requirement hypotheses with supported randomized effects; RQ2 determines the
scope, specificity, functionality, and actionability of those effects; RQ3
measures how efficiently the representation and selector discover such
hypotheses under a fixed budget; and RQ4 evaluates experts' perceived utility
of the resulting evidence records.

All computational methods receive the same discovery and held-out task
manifests, generator-model and request-randomness policy, independent security
Oracle and functional contract, candidate budget, family-valid requirement
protocol, randomization, outcome encoding, semantic-cluster uncertainty, and
multiplicity policy. Candidate universes, rankings, expected directions,
realization policies, and decision rules are frozen before held-out outcomes.
The primary confirmatory analysis is hypothesis- and generator-model-specific
semantic-task-clustered assigned-arm ITT on oracle-evaluable secure-code yield.
Every committed assignment remains in its assigned-arm denominator;
target-change, semantic compliance, generation success, and non-target drift
remain diagnostics.

\subsection{Study Data}

The task inventory draws from SecurityEval, LLMSecEval, SALLM, CWEval,
CyberSecEval, and SeCodePLT, with BaxBench used for backend replication
\cite{siddiq2022securityeval,tony2023llmseceval,siddiq2024sallm,
peng2025cweval,bhatt2023cyberseceval,nie2025secodeplt,vero2025baxbench}.
The frozen task manifest identifies the exact records and preserves dataset
provenance and semantic-cluster-disjoint discovery and evaluation splits. The
frozen requirement manifest binds each result row to one context query,
requirement, ADD or REMOVE operation, prompt-policy realization distribution,
generator model, outcome, and multiplicity family.

\subsection{RQ1: Which Prompt-Side Requirements Have Supported Effects?}

\textbf{RQ1. Which frozen prompt-side security requirements show supported
randomized effects on oracle-evaluable secure-code yield?}

For every requirement hypothesis and generator model, RQ1 reports the
target-minus-no-op assigned-arm ITT risk difference, its multiplicity-adjusted
interval, and the effect status defined in
Section~\ref{sec:matter-definition}. ADD and REMOVE remain distinct hypotheses:
a positive supported ADD effect and a negative supported REMOVE effect are
both evidence that the requirement is beneficial under the corresponding
operation, whereas bidirectional support requires both separately randomized
directions. No unsupported cross-hypothesis average replaces the
hypothesis-specific rows.

The primary outcome is oracle-evaluable secure-code yield. Code validity,
Oracle support, unknown coverage, terminal no-code rate, functionality, and
secure-and-functional joint success remain separate outcomes so that changes
in production or measurement coverage cannot masquerade as security effects.

\subsection{RQ2: When Are Supported Effects Actionable?}

\textbf{RQ2. Under which contexts, operations, policy realizations, and
generator models are requirement effects target-specific and
functionality-preserving?}

RQ2 keeps the RQ1 effect estimate intact and adds evidence axes rather than
collapsing them into one score. Target-versus-placebo and, when family-valid,
target-versus-generic contrasts assess whether the effect is attributable to
the named requirement rather than rewriting, presentation length, or generic
security cues. Functionality is evaluated independently under the frozen
functional contract. A requirement is labeled practically actionable only
when its effect is beneficial, specificity is \textsc{Supported}, and
functionality is \textsc{Preserved}; otherwise the three component statuses
remain visible.

Context, operation, realization, and generator-model comparisons follow only
their prospectively frozen support rules. Realization heterogeneity and model
replication can narrow claim scope but cannot promote the primary effect
status. Target-change, semantic-compliance, non-target-drift, task improvement,
harm, and tie rates explain policy realization without filtering ITT.

\subsection{RQ3: Discovery Efficiency under a Fixed Budget}

\textbf{RQ3. Under a fixed top-$K$ evaluation budget, how much do requirement
selection and context-conditioned representation contribute to discovering
supported effects?}

\subsubsection{Selector Utility}

The selector-only comparison gives \model, Pearson correlation, elastic-net
logistic regression, LLM-based explanation, and seeded random ranking the same
candidate universe, discovery information, and $K$ slots. The unique union of
the methods' top-$K$ skeletons crosses the common bridge once, so a shared
hypothesis is protocolized and randomized once and its evidence is reused by
all selectors that chose it. Empty, bridge-failed, and protocolization-failed
slots remain in denominator $K$. The primary selector endpoint is
Supported-Effect Yield@$K$; Supported-Expected and Supported-Opposite counts
are also reported so discovery quality is not conflated with effect direction.
Held-out outcomes never rerank selectors or replace failed slots.

A separate native-system track evaluates Ji et al.'s causality-aided
prompt--code analysis and NLPerturbator in their native candidate spaces
\cite{ji2025causality,chen2026nlperturbator}. Their outputs cross the same
catalog bridge before randomized evaluation and follow the fixed funnel

\[
K \rightarrow N_{\mathrm{native}} \rightarrow N_{\mathrm{mapped}}
\rightarrow N_{\mathrm{protocol}} \rightarrow N_{\mathrm{randomized}}
\rightarrow N_{\mathrm{supported}}.
\]

This track measures system compatibility, not pure selector superiority;
mapping coverage and mapped yield cannot replace the shared-universe primary
comparison.

\subsubsection{Representation Utility}

The representation comparison holds the selector, budget, bridge, arm
policies, outcomes, and inference fixed while comparing a direct-actionable-
feature universe with the context-conditioned Prompt TSG universe. Candidate
identities and eligible populations may differ, so this is an end-to-end
representation comparison. It reports candidate coverage, applicability,
invalid-operation rate, protocolization, randomized coverage, and
Supported-Effect Yield@$K$. The relevant advantage is whether context
conditioning turns broad security labels into applicable, task-preserving,
evaluable requirement operations.

\subsection{RQ4: Expert-Perceived Utility of Requirement Evidence}

\textbf{RQ4. How do security experts rate and rank the perceived quality and
usefulness of requirement evidence records produced by different methods?}

For \model, each record is rendered as an expert-facing explanation containing
the context, requirement operation, observational selection trace, randomized
effect and interval, specificity, functionality, and claim scope. Every
method's explanation uses the same anonymous wrapper and a balanced
case--method assignment. Qualified experts rate clarity, evidence sufficiency,
credibility, actionability, and overall usefulness on five-point ordinal
scales. Overall usefulness is the preregistered primary human-study outcome;
the other dimensions are secondary.

The primary analysis uses an ordinal mixed-effects model with method as a fixed
effect and participant and case as random effects, followed by
multiplicity-adjusted comparisons. RQ4 concerns perceived utility only: it
does not establish objective requirement identification, repair accuracy, or
validity of the computational effect estimates.
