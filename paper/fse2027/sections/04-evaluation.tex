\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Overview}

The evaluation asks whether requirement-level causal analysis produces useful
evidence and whether experts find the resulting explanations useful for prompt
modification. RQ1 compares the hypotheses identified by five methods. RQ2
isolates the contribution of FCI-based admission. RQ3 examines the effects and
limits of the proposed modifications, and RQ4 compares the perceived utility
of the five methods' explanation products. The computational and human studies
address complementary claims; neither substitutes for the other.

Development, independent qualification, discovery, and held-out confirmation
use prospectively separated task units. Methods share frozen task manifests,
generator and request-randomness policies, candidate budgets, a common
hypothesis bridge, randomized confirmation, and independent measurement.
Candidate identities, method slots, editing policies, and inference rules are
fixed before confirmation outcomes. Each unique selected model-bound effect
is confirmed once and its result is reused by every referring method slot.

\paragraph{Tasks and experimental settings.}
Natural programming tasks must specify an interpretable operation, the relevant
security boundary, and the non-target behavior to preserve. Incomplete functional
specifications limit functionality and joint-success claims without automatically
excluding an independently measurable security comparison. Source lineage and
task-unit deduplication govern the role split. The final source inventory,
admitted language and task families, model configurations, task and request
counts, and budget $K$ require a joint freeze with Oracle qualification and
the comparator implementations before formal execution. This section specifies
the evaluation design; it does not report completed experiments.

\subsection{Compared Methods}
\label{sec:compared-methods}

RQ1 and RQ4 use the same five-method comparison. It includes representative
research approaches and simple reference baselines, rather than treating every
comparator as a state-of-the-art system.

\paragraph{\model.}
Our method constructs task-grounded requirement hypotheses, applies FCI-based
admission and observational RD ranking, and links selected prompt policies to
independent effect evidence. Its explanation identifies the requirement's
scope, the tested modification, and the action or limitation supported by that
evidence.

\paragraph{Prompt Attribution.}
This simple correlation-based baseline uses associations between prompt-side
features and measured outcomes to identify candidate requirements. Its native
evidence is associational: a high attribution score does not establish the
effect of editing the corresponding requirement.

\paragraph{Causal Prompt Concepts.}
This comparator represents the concept-level causal-analysis approach. Its
concept-based candidates must be mapped to a specific task scope, requirement,
and editing operation before they can enter the shared intervention evaluation.
The name is a study working label; the exact source method, implementation,
and adaptation remain to be fixed, rather than being assumed from a related
paper's capabilities.

\paragraph{Prompt Perturbation.}
This comparator represents analysis through natural-language prompt changes.
Its proposed changes must be mapped to a single scoped security requirement
and checked for preservation of non-target behavior. The permitted
perturbations, selection rule, source implementation, and explanation procedure
remain to be fixed. General robustness perturbations are not automatically
valid requirement interventions.

\paragraph{LLM-Judge Explanation.}
This simple baseline directly prompts an LLM to propose a security explanation
and suggested prompt modification from its allowed inputs. The model, prompt,
accessible evidence, and candidate selection rule are fixed prospectively.
Here, \emph{Judge} names the baseline, not the security Oracle or the experts
participating in RQ4; its statements do not adjudicate the other methods' results.

All five methods receive the same $K$ output slots. A common, outcome-blind
bridge maps their outputs into the frozen admissible requirement-policy space.
It preserves each method's proposed requirement and edit rather than supplying
missing reasoning or selecting a better hypothesis on its behalf. Unmappable
outputs remain failed slots. Each method's allowed information, implementation
version, adaptation, and mapping rules must be declared and qualified before
the comparison. Differences in native output spaces make this an evaluation
of the declared method-plus-bridge procedures, not an unqualified comparison
of their original systems. Shared confirmation is an external evaluator, not
a native causal capability credited to every baseline. Equal $K$ also does not
imply equal computation; method-specific model calls and other resource costs
are reported separately. RD-only is reserved for RQ2, not a sixth main comparator.

\subsection{RQ1: Overall Effectiveness}

\textbf{RQ1. How effectively does \model identify actionable prompt-side security hypotheses compared with state-of-the-art methods?}

RQ1 asks which methods identify requirement modifications that receive meaningful
effect evidence in held-out confirmation. The reporting funnel contains $K$,
\emph{Mapped}, \emph{Effect-confirmed}, and \emph{Effect-confirmed Yield@$K$}.
Mapped counts slots whose output resolves to a concrete requirement, task scope,
editing operation, and comparator under the common bridge. Mapping does not
establish that every task can be protocolized or that the proposed change has
an effect.

Effect-confirmed counts slots with a positive-meaningful or negative-meaningful
held-out effect under the practical margins and simultaneous intervals in
Section~\ref{sec:method-causal}. Thus, Effect-confirmed Yield@$K$ is this count
divided by the original $K$; it is the reporting name for meaningful Yield@$K$,
not a new significance rule. Positive and negative counts are reported separately.
Empty slots, mapping or protocolization failures, and non-evaluable findings
remain in the denominator, alongside practically null and inconclusive effects.
The full funnel also reports candidate overlap and failure composition.

An effect-confirmed hypothesis is not necessarily a successful recommendation.
Confirmation establishes a scoped effect of the tested edit, not the correctness
of its explanation or predicted direction. An effect opposite to the proposed
advice can therefore count as effect-bearing while contradicting that advice.
RQ3 examines this distinction; RQ4 evaluates explanation utility. Yield
differences describe the frozen benchmark and do not establish expected
superiority over arbitrary discovery populations.

\subsection{RQ2: Component Contribution}

\textbf{RQ2. Which components of \model contribute to its effectiveness in identifying actionable security hypotheses?}

RQ2 isolates the contribution of FCI-based admission through Full versus
RD-only. The two selectors hold the candidate universe, task rows, natural-state
support, folds, first-order RD scores, tie-breaking, and $K$ fixed; only Full
applies the qualified FCI adjacency gate. Both use the same bridge and downstream
confirmation results.

We report Full-minus-RD-only Effect-confirmed Yield@$K$, selected-candidate
overlap, and empty-slot and failure composition. This comparison tests the
admission criterion, not every representation or measurement component.
In particular, it cannot establish that graph-grounded explanations are easier
to understand; that attribution would require a separately designed comparison.

\subsection{RQ3: Prompt Hardening Effectiveness}

\textbf{RQ3. Can security explanations produced by \model guide prompt hardening that reduces insecure code generation?}

RQ3 examines what the effects used in RQ1 support about the proposed actions.
It uses the same held-out comparisons, not a second experiment selected after
observing favorable findings. The primary contrast is a single-requirement
target edit versus operation-matched no-op on oracle-evaluable secure-code
yield. Placebo/sham and generic-security controls assess specificity under
separately frozen secondary inference rules. Requirement addition (ADD) and
removal (REMOVE) remain distinct policies.

Effects are model-specific, realization-weighted assigned-arm ITT estimates,
with task-unit uncertainty and one simultaneous primary family. Every committed
assignment remains in its assigned arm regardless of fidelity, compliance,
generation success, or non-target drift. We report effect direction, magnitude,
uncertainty, and positive-meaningful, negative-meaningful, practically null,
inconclusive, or non-evaluable status. Validity, evaluability, insecure and
unknown labels, functionality, joint success, and coverage sensitivity bounds
accompany the primary endpoint.

These records distinguish a supported modification, an endpoint-specific warning,
and a decision for which adoption advice must be withheld under
Section~\ref{sec:method-guidance}. Secure-yield gains alone do not establish
reduced underlying insecurity or preserved functionality. RQ3 therefore evaluates
the evidence for the policies underlying the guidance, not whether a person
can read an explanation and successfully apply its advice.

\subsection{RQ4: Explanation Quality}

\textbf{RQ4. How do security experts evaluate the quality of explanations produced by \model and competing methods?}

RQ4 compares anonymized English \emph{Explanation + Suggested Action} records
from the same five methods. Each record is a complete product: the requirement
identified, the evidence presented, and the suggested modification or stated
reason to withhold advice. Common presentation fields make the products
comparable without inventing evidence, strengthening a baseline's claims,
or rewriting every method's output through \model. Each method's evidence
access and explanation-construction procedure, including whether shared
confirmation results are available, must be fixed before producing study
materials. RQ1's external validation does not automatically become a baseline's
own explanation evidence.

Experts rate five items on 1--5 Likert scales: clarity, evidence
sufficiency/justification, credibility, actionability, and overall usefulness.
The planned primary rating is overall usefulness; the other four characterize
the perceived strengths and weaknesses of each product. This is a comparison
of complete-product perceived utility, not a wording-only comparison with
identical evidence. Differences cannot be attributed solely to Prompt TSG,
causal correctness, or prose format.

The study will use balanced case--method assignment and blinded randomized
presentation order. Prospective case sampling must include unfavorable and
unresolved findings rather than only successful hardening examples. Study
materials must also follow prespecified handling of empty or unmappable outputs
and unavailable explanation products, with decisions and counts reported by
method. Participant
criteria and counts, case sampling, comparator materials, exact item wording
and anchors, ethics determination, and an analysis accounting for repeated
ratings by expert and case require preregistration before recruitment or
response inspection. The primary rating and secondary-item multiplicity rule
must be finalized in that plan.

Rated credibility does not establish causal correctness, and rated clarity
or actionability does not establish objective comprehension, actual adoption,
or repair accuracy. These ratings cannot revise the computational evidence in
RQ1--RQ3. They assess whether the resulting explanations help experts judge
the proposed prompt changes and their limits.
